Six weeks into building seriously with AI, the system started teaching me something I hadn't read anywhere.

I was asking a frontier model to write code, run searches, read files, generate reports — dozens of operations per session, each one consuming context window. The work was getting done. The outputs were good. But something felt structurally off in a way I couldn't articulate at first. Sessions were expensive. The model always seemed slightly taxed. And the further I got into a session, the more I noticed quality drift — not in the model's raw capability, but in how much of its attention was actually focused on the hard problem versus the plumbing.

Then the reframe arrived: the model's context is the bottleneck. Not its execution capacity.

Every time I asked a frontier model to handle something a cheaper, faster process could do — scan a directory, classify a document, fetch structured data, run a cron-style task — I was spending the scarcest resource on labor. The context window that should be reserved for judgment and synthesis was filling up with retrieval and formatting work.

I had an orchestra and I was asking the conductor to play second violin.

The Conductor Doesn't Play Every Instrument

A conductor's job is not to be the best musician in the room. It's to hold the full score in mind, shape the interpretation, notice when the timing is off, decide when to push and when to pull back, and bring a coherent vision out of dozens of independent performers who each only see their own part.

The moment the conductor picks up a bow and starts playing, they've lost the view. The ensemble still makes sound. But nobody's watching the whole thing anymore.

AI orchestration works the same way. A frontier model's edge — the thing you're actually paying for — is judgment: knowing which sub-problem matters, what quality looks like, where the output is structurally wrong, how disparate inputs fit together into something coherent. That edge degrades in direct proportion to how much raw execution you load it with.

The principle: reserve the model's context for judgment, synthesis, and review. Delegate everything else.

This sounds obvious when stated plainly. It's surprisingly rare in practice.

The Three Tiers — Or: The Household, the Field, and the Crown

Medieval estates ran on a hierarchy that's worth borrowing. Not because AI is feudal — but because the division of labor was genuinely well-designed for the problem of coordinating complex work across agents with wildly different capabilities and costs.

The Seneschal managed the household: inventory, scheduling, correspondence, the ten thousand small tasks that kept the estate running without anyone having to think about them. The Seneschal didn't advise on military strategy or draft treaties. The Seneschal made sure the grain stores were counted and the carts left on time. Reliable, tireless, and critically — invisible when working correctly.

In a modern AI stack, this is your batch and scheduled tier. Feed scanning, document classification, nightly data collection, templated report generation, pattern extraction, model benchmarking — any task that is high-volume, predictable in format, and low in required judgment. A small local model (7 to 14 billion parameters, running on commodity hardware you probably already own) handles this work well. Cost: near-zero. Required human attention during operation: near-zero. The Seneschal works while you sleep and leaves structured outputs on your desk by morning.

The Marshal commanded field operations: enforcement, execution in the physical world, coordination of things that required presence and movement rather than counsel. The Marshal didn't sit in the war room drawing maps. The Marshal went where the action was.

In the AI stack, this is your hardware-access and interactive tier — the agent that needs a stable browser session, a physical radio receiver, a running desktop application, or direct human interaction. Tasks requiring hardware interfaces, browser automation, always-on monitoring of live data streams, or execution in contexts where a scheduled batch job can't operate. The Marshal is capable but costs more than the Seneschal, and shouldn't be called upon when the Seneschal can handle it.

The Chancellor was the sovereign's closest adviser: the person in the room when the hard decisions were made. Draft this treaty. Assess this risk. What does this intelligence actually mean? The Chancellor synthesized complex inputs, reasoned under uncertainty, and produced counsel that the sovereign could act on with confidence.

This is your frontier model — the expensive tier, the one whose context window is the scarcest resource in the system. Long-chain planning, architectural decisions, quality writing, synthesis across disparate inputs, judgment calls where being right at the margin actually matters. The Chancellor doesn't scan documents. The Chancellor reads what the Seneschal has already distilled and advises on what to do about it.

The design question for any task: what is the least senior role that can handle this at the required quality level? Routing to the Chancellor when the Seneschal could handle it is waste. Routing to the Seneschal when the work requires counsel is garbage that the Chancellor will have to clean up later — spending context on a task it should never have seen.

The Routing Decision

In practice, routing decisions follow a short decision tree.

Is the task schedulable? If it runs on a clock rather than requiring real-time human judgment to initiate, it belongs with the Seneschal. Dev journals, RSS ingestion, weekly summaries, benchmark runs, data refreshes — these never need to touch a live frontier session.

Is the task I/O-bound or judgment-bound? I/O-bound means the hard part is retrieving, formatting, or transforming data. Judgment-bound means the hard part is deciding what it means or what to do about it. I/O-bound routes to the Seneschal. Judgment-bound routes to the Chancellor.

Does the task require hardware access, session stability, or live interaction that a scheduled process can't provide? Route to the Marshal. Browser automation, hardware interfaces, and live human-directed sessions belong here — not in a frontier session that charges by the token.

Does the task require quality at the margin — where a worse output would change a real decision? This is where the Chancellor earns the cost. Architecture decisions, final-form writing, synthesis that someone will act on. The places where being right matters enough to justify the expense.

Everything else is noise in the conductor's ear.

The Failure Modes

Before building the delegation layer, I had a single-tier system: ask the frontier model for everything. The failure modes were instructive.

Context pollution: A session that starts with ten file reads and three web searches has consumed 20–30% of its useful context window before any real reasoning has happened. The Chancellor can still perform — but is working with less headroom for the hard problem. Quality at the margins suffers first, and it's subtle enough that you don't notice until you compare.

False efficiency: A session feels productive because operations are completing. But "operations completing" is not the same as "judgment being applied." Asking the Chancellor to count the grain stores is completing an operation. It is not a good use of the Chancellor's time.

Monotask trap: Without a delegation layer, every task — trivial or consequential — demands your presence to initiate and monitor. The system scales linearly with your attention. You become the bottleneck rather than the conductor.

Cost surprise: Work that runs daily — feed scanning, data collection, report generation — accumulates API costs that look small per-session but compound fast. A frontier model doing overnight batch work that a Seneschal-tier local model could handle is burning budget at a 10–50x cost premium for no quality advantage. The Seneschal doesn't mind working nights. The Chancellor bills by the hour.

What the Delegation Layer Looks Like in Practice

The delegation layer doesn't have to be complex. It has to be intentional.

For recurring work: Define the task type, the expected output format, and the acceptable quality floor. If a task passes all three, hand it to the Seneschal and stop touching it yourself. The test: can you write down exactly what "done" looks like? If yes, it's automatable.

For retrieval in live sessions: When a task requires reading multiple files, running parallel searches, or collecting data from several sources, use subagent calls rather than doing it inline. The Chancellor holds the reasoning. Subagents handle retrieval. Results return for synthesis. The frontier model's context stays clean for the judgment step — the part you're actually paying for.

For session startup: Read context files and batch process outputs before the Chancellor session begins, or pass them in as pre-staged structured inputs. Don't spend session time on data collection that the Seneschal could have run the night before. When you sit down with the Chancellor, come with the briefing already prepared.

For output review: The Seneschal produces a draft or structured result. The Chancellor reviews, edits, decides. Generate cheaply; judge expensively. The conductor reviews what the ensemble rehearsed — not the raw recordings of every individual practice session.

The Compounding Effect

Here's what six weeks of incrementally building the delegation layer produced: each task that moved to the Seneschal became attention budget recovered for something harder. Each task that went to the Marshal freed the Chancellor for pure synthesis. Sessions got shorter and sharper. The work brought to live sessions was genuinely judgment-heavy — planning, architecture, final-form writing — because everything retrievable, schedulable, and formattable had somewhere else to go.

The system got smarter as the Chancellor had to do less.

This is the counterintuitive core of the Orchestrator Principle: the Chancellor's value is not proportional to how busy it is. It's proportional to how well the surrounding system has protected its attention. A Chancellor who also has to file correspondence, count inventory, and monitor the perimeter never has time to actually advise on the decisions that matter.

Organize the household correctly and the Chancellor's full attention is on the work that deserves it.

Starting from Scratch

If you're building this from the beginning, the sequence matters.

First: audit your current sessions for Seneschal-eligible work. Anything you do on a schedule, anything with a predictable output format, anything that's I/O-bound — flag it. This is your batch backlog, and it probably represents 40–60% of what your frontier model is currently doing.

Second: build one automated batch job before adding any other tier. The discipline of defining "what does done look like" for a scheduled task teaches the routing language you'll need for everything else. Start small: a nightly summary, a weekly report, a data refresh. Get the Seneschal working. Learn what it feels like to wake up to a desk that's already organized.

Third: introduce subagent patterns for retrieval in your live sessions. The rule of thumb: if a session needs data from more than two sources, those fetches belong in subagent calls, not inline operations. The Chancellor reads the brief; the brief was assembled by someone else.

Fourth: protect the Chancellor's early-session window. The opening of a session — before context fills — is when reasoning is sharpest. Pre-stage inputs overnight so that first slot is pure synthesis, not setup. The Chancellor walks in already briefed.

The goal is not a complex court. The goal is a correctly ordered one.

The Structural Insight

What this practice reveals is an attention economy for AI systems. The most valuable resource in any multi-agent setup isn't compute or API quota — it's the Chancellor's context window, applied precisely to problems only the Chancellor can solve.

Every operation you move to the Seneschal is budget recovered. Every subagent call that handles retrieval and returns only the result is the system learning to protect its own most expensive resource. Every task that runs on a schedule without touching a live session is the household managing itself so counsel can focus on what requires counsel.

Burning Chancellor-tier context on Seneschal-tier work is the equivalent of summoning your senior adviser to count the grain stores. Technically capable. Structurally absurd.

Six weeks in, the richest learning wasn't about prompt engineering or model capability. It was about court design: who handles what, who reports to whom, and what the Chancellor never has to see — precisely because the Seneschal already took care of it.

Stop coding. Start conducting.
