Games We Passed On

The more interesting story this week is what we didn't bet — and why.

When we ran the full model against this week's schedule, we identified over two dozen matchups where our Elo-based projections showed meaningful disagreement with the expected outcome. UConn versus Seton Hall, for instance, shows the model projecting a 12-point UConn advantage. Virginia Tech visiting North Carolina looks like an 11.4-point edge for the Tar Heels. BYU at West Virginia shows a 6.3-point gap in West Virginia's favor, flagged at high confidence.

We passed on all of them.

The reason is structural, not analytical: for the vast majority of high-gap games on our radar, the market hasn't published lines yet. A model disagreement is only actionable when there's a market price to disagree with. When the market line shows a flat zero across the board — as it did for nearly every high-confidence game this week — we're looking at a signal we can identify but not act on. The model sees something. The market simply hasn't spoken yet.

This is a recurring feature of early-in-the-week analysis. Lines materialize closer to tip, and when they do, we'll see whether the market converges toward our projections or prices them differently. If BYU-West Virginia opens at West Virginia -6 and our model says -6.3, that's not a bet. If it opens at pick'em, that's a different conversation.

For now, those games go into the watchlist and wait for market publication.

Methodology Note

The model operates on Elo ratings calibrated to current season performance, adjusted for home-court advantage and recent form. We generate a projected spread for each game and compare it against the available market line. The gap between model output and market price is our primary signal. We only take action when that gap exceeds a minimum threshold, and we scale stakes to confidence tier — high-confidence calls receive full unit allocation, medium confidence receives 1.0x, low confidence receives 0.75x or less.

This is a model validation exercise. We are testing whether Elo-based projections generate predictive signal against market prices over a meaningful sample. Seven weeks of data is insufficient to draw durable conclusions; thirty or more would start to be informative. We are, genuinely, still building toward that sample.
